# Evidence on cross-model review vs self-review, iteration counts, AI code review quality, plan-first workflows, and VLM screenshot review

Scope note: research conducted 2026-10-07. "Strong" = peer-reviewed or large-N with controls; "weak" = small-N, single codebase, or secondary summary; "vendor" = published by the company selling or operating the tool. Where a number came from a search-engine snippet of a paper rather than the paper text I fetched, it is marked "(snippet)".

## KQ1: Self-correction and self-review — under what conditions does it help, and when does it hurt?

### Takeaway
Intrinsic self-correction (the same model critiques and revises its own output with no external signal) does not reliably improve reasoning or code and frequently degrades it; documented gains require an external signal (unit tests, tools, a stronger or different model, humans) or dedicated RL training. Documented failure modes are over-correction (flipping correct answers to wrong ones), feedback that is too generic, and reviewers fabricating findings once real errors are exhausted.

### Cited Findings
- Huang et al. (ICLR 2024), "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet": with intrinsic self-correction, GPT-3.5-Turbo fell on GSM8K 75.9% -> 75.1% (round 1) -> 74.7% (round 2), CommonSenseQA 75.8% -> 38.1% -> 41.8%, HotpotQA 26.0% -> 25.0% -> 25.0%; GPT-4 fell on GSM8K 95.5% -> 91.5% -> 89.0%, CommonSenseQA 82.0% -> 79.5% -> 80.0%, HotpotQA 49.0% -> 49.0% -> 43.0%. On GSM8K GPT-3.5 kept its answer 74.7% of the time and, when it changed, was "more likely to modify a correct answer to an incorrect one than to revise an incorrect answer". Prior positive results (Reflexion, RCI) depended on oracle ground-truth labels (GPT-3.5 GSM8K 75.9% -> 84.3% with oracle), and some reported gains came from weak initial prompts (constrained generation: a better prompt scored 81.8% vs prior work's 53.0%, after which self-correction lowered it to 75.1%). Strong. — [arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798); full text via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.01798)
- Same paper, Table 7 (GSM8K, GPT-3.5): standard 76.7%; self-consistency with 3 responses 82.5%; multi-agent debate round 1 (6 responses) 83.2% vs self-consistency with 6 responses 85.3%; debate round 2 (9 responses) 83.0% vs self-consistency with 9 responses 88.2%. Same-model debate underperformed simple majority voting at equal cost. Strong. — [arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)
- Madaan et al. (2023), Self-Refine: single LLM as generator, feedback provider and refiner; ~20% absolute average improvement across 7 tasks with GPT-3.5, ChatGPT, GPT-4. But math reasoning barely moved (GPT-4 92.9% -> 93.1%) because "a consistent-looking reasoning chain can deceive LLMs to think that 'everything looks good'"; with oracle error detection the gain rose (GPT-3 +4.8%, GPT-4 +0.7%). Feedback specificity mattered: on Sentiment Reversal, specific feedback 43.2% vs generic 31.2% vs none 0%. Vicuna-13B could not produce usable feedback; a mixed setup (Vicuna generates, ChatGPT refines) lifted math from 24.18% to 40.5%. Strong for the positive tasks; the paper's own analysis shows weak-model and reasoning limits. — [arXiv 2303.17651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651); full text via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.17651)
- Shinn et al. (2023), Reflexion: 91% pass@1 on HumanEval vs 80% for GPT-4 baseline, but the coding signal was self-generated unit tests (up to 6 sampled tests), AlfWorld ran up to 12 trials with "an immediate spike in the improvement between the first two trials", and the authors note self-correction "is an emergent quality of stronger, larger models". Strong but the HumanEval number depends on executable test feedback, not pure introspection. — [arXiv 2303.11366](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366); full text via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.11366)
- Gou et al. (ICLR 2024), CRITIC: tool-interactive critiquing (search, interpreters, toxicity APIs) consistently improved QA, math program synthesis and toxicity reduction; the authors stress "the crucial importance of external feedback". Strong. — [arXiv 2305.11738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738)
- Kamoi et al. (TACL 2024), survey "When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?": "no prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs" outside tasks exceptionally suited to it; self-correction works with reliable external feedback or large-scale fine-tuning; prior studies often used "unfair evaluations that over-evaluate self-correction". Strong (survey). — [arXiv 2406.01297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)
- Olausson et al. (ICLR 2024), "Is Self-Repair a Silver Bullet for Code Generation?": with repair cost counted, gains were "often modest, vary a lot between subsets of the data, and are sometimes not present at all"; GPT-4 beat i.i.d. sampling by up to 8% on APPS, GPT-3.5 by up to 3% on HumanEval, CodeLlama-13b-instruct not at all; GPT-3.5 gained up to 34% on competition-level APPS problems but nothing on introductory ones; at fixed budgets, more initial diverse samples usually beat extensive repair. Replacing GPT-4's self-feedback with human feedback raised success >1.57x. Strong. — [arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896); full text [HTML v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09896v4)
- Kumar et al. (Google DeepMind, 2024), SCoRe: multi-turn online RL on self-generated data gave Gemini 1.0 Pro / 1.5 Flash +15.6% (MATH) and +9.1% (HumanEval) self-correction gains; prompting/SFT approaches suffer "distribution mismatch" and "behavior collapse" toward minimal edits. Strong, but requires training, not prompting. — [arXiv 2409.12917](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12917)
- Song et al. (ICLR 2025), "Mind the Gap": self-improvement is governed by a "generation-verification gap" that "scales monotonically with the model pre-training flops", so weaker models verify their own output worse. Strong (theory + experiments). — [arXiv 2412.02674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02674)
- Sharma et al. (2023, v4 2025), sycophancy: across five state-of-the-art assistants, "both humans and preference models prefer convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time", and preference-model optimization "sometimes sacrifices truthfulness in favor of sycophancy" — the mechanism by which an author model capitulates to a wrong reviewer finding. Strong. — [arXiv 2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
- "More Rounds, More Noise" (2026): separating production and review into independent sessions (single-pass cross-context review) scored F1 0.376; multi-turn review variants scored F1 0.303 (p<0.001) and 0.263. Multi-turn review raised recall by 0.08 but produced 62% more false positives (8.5 vs 5.2), collapsing precision from 0.30 to 0.20, via "false positive pressure" (reviewers fabricate findings when legitimate errors are exhausted) and "review target drift" (critiquing the conversation instead of the artifact). Preprint; moderate strength. — [arXiv 2603.16244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16244)
- "Can LLMs Correct Themselves? CorrectBench" (2025): self-correction helps complex reasoning, mixing strategies helps further at efficiency cost, but reasoning LLMs such as DeepSeek-R1 "have limited optimization under additional self-correction methods and have high time costs" and a plain CoT baseline is competitive. Preprint. — [arXiv 2510.16062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16062)
- Anthropic, "Building effective agents" (Dec 2024): the evaluator-optimizer loop ("one LLM call generates a response while another provides evaluation and feedback in a loop") is recommended only "when we have clear evaluation criteria, and when iterative refinement provides measurable value", and when "the LLM can provide such feedback itself". Vendor guidance. — [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)

### Inferences
- Self-review by the same model in the same context is the weakest configuration in the literature; every robust gain comes from a signal the author did not generate itself (tests, tools, a different/stronger model, a human). A review loop should therefore be anchored on executable checks plus an external reviewer, not on the author re-reading its diff.
- Over-correction is a first-class risk: the author model is more likely to change a correct answer than fix a wrong one when prompted to reconsider, and it is trained toward agreeing with pushback. Any loop must let the author reject findings with evidence and let tests arbitrate.
- Multi-turn back-and-forth between author and reviewer in one conversation is worse than independent fresh-context passes; the project's choice to run the reviewer as a separate harness with its own context is consistent with the evidence.

### Gaps
- A search snippet attributed an "accuracy-correction paradox" (models with base accuracy in the mid-60s to mid-70s benefit modestly; stronger models are systematically harmed) to the 2025-2026 self-correction literature, but I could not confirm which paper states it; treat as unverified.
- No study isolates self-review of agent-written repository-scale diffs (as opposed to single-function benchmarks); the closest are Olausson (HumanEval/APPS) and the SWE-bench patch-quality papers in KQ4.

## KQ2: Self-preference and same-model judging; does a heterogeneous reviewer reduce it, and does any study measure bug detection when reviewer != author?

### Takeaway
Self-preference bias in LLM judges is well replicated, scales with self-recognition, extends to same-family models, and persists even on objectively verifiable code rubrics; verifiers from a different model family accept fewer wrong answers than self- or same-family verifiers. Direct, controlled measurement of bug-detection rates when the code reviewer is a different frontier model than the author is thin: the strongest evidence is indirect (Olausson's GPT-4-reviews-GPT-3.5 result, cross-family verification studies, mixed-model debate) plus one industrial deployment (Uber) that found a Claude generator paired with an OpenAI grader gave the best F1.

### Cited Findings
- Panickssery, Bowman & Feng (2024), "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations": out of the box, GPT-4 distinguished its own summaries from two other LLMs and humans with 73.5% pairwise accuracy (GPT-3.5 ~50-55%, Llama-2-7b ~51%); fine-tuning on 500 examples pushed GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 above 90%; all three evaluators showed self-preference (XSUM, CNN/DailyMail), and "the strength of self-preference bias is linearly correlated with the LLM's self-recognition capability", with fine-tuned self-recognition causally increasing self-preference. Strong. — [arXiv 2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076)
- Wataoka et al. (NeurIPS 2024 Safe GenAI workshop), "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge": GPT-4 shows significant self-preference and assigns "significantly higher evaluations to outputs with lower perplexity than human evaluators"; the bias "exists because LLMs prefer texts more familiar to them", not only texts they authored. Moderate (workshop). — [arXiv 2410.21819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819)
- Zheng et al. (2023), MT-Bench / "Judging LLM-as-a-judge": GPT-4 favored its own answers with "a 10% higher win rate", Claude-v1 with "a 25% higher win rate", GPT-3.5 showed none; the authors caution "our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias" due to limited data. GPT-4 judge agreed with human experts 85% vs 81% human-human agreement. Strong for agreement; weak for bias magnitude. — [arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685); full text via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- "Self-Preference Bias in Rubric-Based Evaluation of LLMs" (2026): on IFEval and LiveCodeBench (programmatically verifiable rubrics), "judges can be more than 50% more likely to incorrectly mark [failed rubrics] as satisfied when the output is their own"; judges "favor outputs produced by themselves or by models from their own family"; on HealthBench the skew reaches 10 points; "Ensembling multiple judges helps mitigate SPB, but without fully eliminating it." Preprint; directly relevant to code. — [arXiv 2604.06996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06996)
- "Quantifying and Mitigating Self-Preference Bias of LLM Judges" (2026): across 20 mainstream LLMs, "advanced capabilities are often uncorrelated, or even negatively correlated, with low SPB"; a structured multi-dimensional evaluation cut SPB by 31.5% on average. Preprint. — [arXiv 2604.22891](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22891)
- "Do LLM Evaluators Prefer Themselves for a Reason?" (2025): across 7 model families on math, factual and code benchmarks, much self-preference tracks genuinely better outputs, but stronger models display "more pronounced harmful self-preference bias when they do err", and a long chain-of-thought before judging reduces the harmful part. Preprint. — [arXiv 2504.03846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03846)
- "When Does Verification Pay Off?" (2025): 37 models from 7 families (Llama3, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1, 0.5B-72B) on 9 benchmarks: "the more similar the solver and verifier distributions, the more likely the verifier is to accept incorrect answers"; cross-family verification "substantially outperforms" self-verification; reasoning-post-trained models show "negligible gains in self-verification" but "substantial improvements in cross-family verification". Preprint; mostly math/logic, not code review. — [arXiv 2512.02304](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02304v2)
- "Too Consistent to Detect" (EMNLP 2025): self-consistent errors are model-specific — even Qwen2.5-7B vs Qwen2.5-14B share only 28.7% of them, with lower overlap across families; consistency-based self-detection falls to AUROC <= 0.5 on them; a cross-model probe using an external verifier LLM's hidden states lifted AUROC 0.7287 -> 0.8289 (SciQ, Llama3.1-8B). Strong. (A snippet cited 9.6% overlap between Qwen2.5-14B and Llama3.1-70B; not confirmed in the text I read.) — [arXiv 2505.17656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17656)
- Du et al. (2023), multi-agent debate: 3 agents, 2 rounds raised GSM8K 77.0% -> 85.0%, arithmetic 67.0% -> 81.8%, MMLU 63.9% -> 71.1%, biography factuality 66.0% -> 73.8%; a mixed-model experiment on 20 GSM8K problems scored Bard 11, ChatGPT 14, joint debate 17 correct; gains plateaued after ~4 rounds. Strong for same-model; the mixed-model result is N=20 (weak). — [arXiv 2305.14325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325); full text via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.14325)
- Chan et al. (2023), ChatEval: multi-agent referee teams with distinct personas align better with human judgments than single-agent judging, and homogeneous role prompts degrade performance. Preprint. — [arXiv 2308.07201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07201)
- Smit et al. (2024), "Should we be going MAD?": debate "do[es] not inherently outperform" self-consistency or ensembling; it is "more sensitive to different hyperparameter settings", especially agreement intensity. Strong. — [arXiv 2311.17371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)
- "Diversity of Thought Elicits Stronger Reasoning in Multi-Agent Debate" (2024/25): after 4 rounds, a heterogeneous trio (Gemini-Pro, Mixtral 8x7B, PaLM 2-M) hit 91% on GSM8K vs 82% for three Gemini-Pro instances, and beat GPT-4; 94% on ASDiv. Preprint; moderate. — [arXiv 2410.12853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12853)
- "Stop Overvaluing Multi-Agent Debate" (2025): 5 MAD methods x 9 benchmarks x 4 models; MAD "often fail[s] to outperform simple single-agent baselines such as Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency, even when consuming significantly more inference-time computation", while "model heterogeneity" is a "universal antidote" that consistently improves MAD. Position paper with experiments. — [arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)
- Olausson et al. (ICLR 2024): when GPT-4 wrote the feedback for GPT-3.5's programs, GPT-3.5 could "break through the performance barrier and become more effective than i.i.d. sampling without repair", which self-feedback could not achieve — the clearest controlled result that a different (stronger) reviewer finds more actionable faults than the author. Strong. — [arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09896v4)
- Uber uReview (Aug 2025, vendor/industrial): the best configuration "paired Anthropic Claude-4-Sonnet as the primary comment generator with OpenAI o4-mini-high as the review grader", achieving the highest F1 of all tested combinations (vs GPT-4.1, o3, o1, Llama-4, DeepSeek R1); engineers mark 75% of posted comments useful and >65% are addressed; it reviews >90% of ~65,000 weekly diffs. — [uber.com](https://www.uber.com/en-GB/blog/ureview/)
- Claude Code best-practices docs (Anthropic, vendor): "A fresh context improves code review since Claude won't be biased toward code it just wrote", with an explicit Writer/Reviewer two-session pattern; a verification subagent "has a fresh model try to refute the result, so the agent doing the work isn't the one grading it". — [code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)

### Inferences
- Self-preference is not only about authorship but familiarity (perplexity) and family: a reviewer from the same vendor/family (e.g., a different Claude model reviewing Claude's code) likely retains part of the bias. Using a different vendor's harness (Claude Code vs Codex) is the configuration the bias literature most supports.
- The benefit of a different reviewer is largest when the author's errors are "self-consistent" (it would make the same mistake again on re-read); cross-model verifiers catch those where self-checks score at chance.
- Debate-style multi-round negotiation between agents is not where the gains are; a single independent review pass by a heterogeneous model, judged against tests, is better supported than long author-reviewer dialogues.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that measures defect-detection rate when a frontier model (e.g., GPT-5/Codex) reviews another frontier model's (e.g., Claude's) repository-scale diff versus same-model review; Uber's result is the only cross-vendor generator/grader datapoint and it is vendor-reported with no public precision table.
- A 2025 study of GPT-4o, Grok-3 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet on a 27-bug C program (ResearchGate) reports chatbots "comparable to human reviewers" but I could not retrieve its full text or pairing design; not relied upon.

## KQ3: Measured quality of AI code review — precision, recall, acceptance, and when noise makes a reviewer not worth keeping

### Takeaway
Field acceptance of AI review comments ranges from ~7-8% (Mozilla/Ubisoft, lightly filtered) to ~40% resolution (Google AutoCommenter), ~65-75% (Uber, aggressively filtered) and 73.8% "resolved" (Beko/Qodo, with 21.3% "won't fix" and a longer PR cycle). Recall on human-flagged review issues is low: 15-31% for eight frontier models on SWE-PRBench. Every industrial team that reports success did so by suppressing low-confidence or low-value categories; the practitioner evidence is that precision, not volume, determines whether developers keep reading.

### Cited Findings
- Google, "Resolving code review comments with ML" (2023): offline, the model proposed edits for 52% of reviewer comments at a 50% precision target; online it suggested for ~50% of relevant comments; 40-50% of previewed suggestions were applied, rising to >70% after UX changes. Vendor/industrial. — [research.google](https://research.google/blog/resolving-code-review-comments-with-ml/)
- Google AutoCommenter (AIware 2024): T5-based best-practice commenter for C++/Java/Python/Go serving "tens of thousands of developers"; required an 80% "useful" ratio before wider rollout, plateaued at ~54% in the first-adopter phase and reached 80% only after suppressing 22 problematic practice URLs; ~40% of comments were resolved by later code changes; only ~10% of comments received explicit feedback; 66% of its top-50 practices "cannot be easily detected by linters"; raters flagged "correct but low-value" comments (e.g., missing punctuation) as a problem. Industrial, peer-reviewed. — [arXiv 2405.13565](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13565); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13565)
- Cihan et al. (ICSE SEIP 2025), "Automated Code Review In Practice" (Beko; Qodo PR-Agent on GPT-4-32K, ~$0.48/PR): 4,335 PRs, 1,568 AI-reviewed; 3.65 comments/PR; 73.8% of comments "Resolved", 21.3% "Won't Fix"; PR closure time rose from 5h52m to 8h20m (p<<0.001) with wide project variance (one project -2h59m, another +10h29m); 68.8% of surveyed developers saw only "minor" quality improvement, average rating 3.46/5; ignored comments were "out-of-scope", "trivial" or "unrelated" ("fix code blocks that are not in the scope of the task"). Strong field study. — [arXiv 2412.18531](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18531); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.18531)
- Mozilla + Ubisoft RevMate (2024): RAG + LLM-as-judge filter; over 587 patch reviews, 8.1% (Mozilla) and 7.2% (Ubisoft) of generated comments were accepted, with another 14.6% / 20.5% marked valuable as tips; refactoring comments were accepted far more (18.2% / 18.6%) than functional ones (4.8% / 5.2%); 36 of 119 accepted comments were edited first; median 43s inspection per patch; accepted comments triggered later revisions as often as human comments (74% vs 73%). Strong field study. — [arXiv 2411.07091](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07091)
- Meta MetaMateCR (2025): AI-generated fixes for review comments on "10s of thousands" of comments weekly; production ActionableToApplied rate 19.7% (+9.2 pp over GPT-4o); offline exact-match 68%; a safety RCT found a 5% increase in review time when patches were shown to reviewers, removed by showing them to authors only. Industrial. — [arXiv 2507.13499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13499)
- Uber uReview (Aug 2025, vendor): 75% of comments marked useful, >65% addressed in the same changeset, median review latency 4 minutes; design rationale: "a high false-positive rate undermines engineers' perception of the tool's accuracy and usefulness — when they encounter many false-positive comments, they start to tune out and ignore them", so entire low-reception categories are suppressed. — [uber.com](https://www.uber.com/en-GB/blog/ureview/)
- Ericsson experience report (ICSME 2025): lightweight LLM + static-analysis reviewer evaluated by expert survey with "encouraging results"; snippet: 9 experts invited, 8 completed, 10 code snippets, mixed positive/negative comments; no acceptance rate published. Weak. — [arXiv 2507.19115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19115)
- OpenAI CriticGPT (2024): GPT-4-family critic trained with RLHF; on naturally occurring ChatGPT code bugs its critiques were preferred over human critiques in 63% of cases (62.7% vs ChatGPT critiques on human-detected bugs); critics "catch more bugs than human contractors paid for code review" yet have "much higher" hallucination/nitpick rates than humans (reduced vs ChatGPT); human+critic teams "catch similar numbers of bugs to LLM critics while hallucinating less"; Force Sampling Beam Search exposes an explicit precision-recall knob. Vendor research, strong design. — [arXiv 2407.00215](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00215); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00215)
- CRQBench (Google, 2024): 100 C++ code-reasoning questions derived from review comments; GPT-4 answered 65 correctly. Small. — [arXiv 2408.08453](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08453)
- SWR-Bench (FSE 2026): 1,000 verified PRs with full project context; hit-based precision/recall/F1 judged by an LLM with ~90% human agreement; "current systems underperform", tools "are more adept at detecting functional errors", and aggregating multiple reviews raised F1 by up to 43.67%. Peer-reviewed benchmark. — [arXiv 2509.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01494)
- SWE-PRBench (2026): 350 PRs with human-annotated issues, LLM judge kappa=0.75; 8 frontier models detected only 15-31% of human-flagged issues on diff-only input; the top four were statistically indistinguishable (mean 0.147-0.153); adding more repository context degraded every model monotonically, and a 2,000-token diff-with-summary prompt beat a 2,500-token full-context prompt ("attention dilution"). Preprint. — [arXiv 2603.26130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26130)
- GitHub, "60 million Copilot code reviews" (Mar 2026, vendor): Copilot performs >1 in 5 code reviews on GitHub; 71% of reviews "surface actionable feedback", 29% post nothing; 5.1 comments per review on average; 12,000+ orgs auto-run it; an agentic redesign raised positive feedback 8.1%, a stronger reasoning model a further 6% at +16% latency. No precision/acceptance figure. — [github.blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/60-million-copilot-code-reviews-and-counting/)
- GitHub ReviewBench (Oct 2026, vendor): 219 PRs from 187 repos in 19 languages, scored on precision ("proportion that are valid"), recall and configurable F-beta; an ensemble change predicted +8.0% addressed rate (precision), +13.6% recall and +227% critical comments, vs +262% observed online. — [github.blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/reviewbench-an-open-benchmark-for-ai-code-review/)
- Practitioner comparison of four tools on one PHP/React codebase (2026, weak): 146 merged PRs, 679 findings; CodeRabbit 281 findings (3.4/PR, 2.3% false positives), Cursor BugBot 128 (2.6/PR, 4.8% FP), Greptile 120 (2.2/PR, 0% FP with the caveat that "uncontested" was counted as valid), Sentry Seer 0% FP at critical (6/6) but 15% at high; 93.4% of findings were unique to a single tool, only 6% overlapped between two, none across all four. — [dev.to](https://dev.to/_vjk/best-ai-code-reviewer-in-2026-we-ran-4-in-parallel-for-3-weeks-146-prs-679-findings-1c0f)
- "3100 Opinions on Code Review in an AI World" (2026): 3,100 coded documents from 38,709 blogs/threads; agent-authored PRs are "reviewed less often, merged several times faster, and discussed less than human-authored ones"; "review is the control point through which a coding agent's effect on software is decided". Grey-literature synthesis. — [arXiv 2607.07980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07980)
- "Comparing AI Coding Agents: PR acceptance" (2026, snippet): acceptance of agent PRs — Codex 79.9%, Cursor 74.4%, Claude Code 72.6%, Devin 68.0%, Copilot 68.0%; Copilot PRs drew 4.94 reviews/PR vs 1.39 for Codex. — [arXiv 2602.08915](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08915v2)
- DORA 2024: a 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with a 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and a 7.2% decrease in delivery stability, attributed partly to larger batch sizes that are "slower to review". Survey-based. — [dora.dev](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/); [Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report)
- Claude Code docs (vendor): "A reviewer prompted to find gaps will usually report some, even when the work is sound ... Chasing every finding leads to over-engineering ... Tell the reviewer to flag only gaps that affect correctness or the stated requirements, and treat the rest as optional." — [code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)

### Inferences
- Acceptance rates are mostly a function of filtering policy, not model quality: the same class of tool spans 7% to 75% acceptance depending on how aggressively low-value categories are suppressed. The industrial consensus threshold is high — Google would not roll out below 80% "useful", Uber optimized F1 with a second-model grader — so a "must-fix-only" policy is the norm among deployments that survived.
- Functional/bug findings are both the most valuable and the least often accepted (Mozilla 5%); style/refactor findings are accepted more but add little. A loop that keeps only must-fix findings will see few findings per round, which is expected and desirable.
- Low recall (15-31%) means AI review is a complement to tests, not a substitute; "no findings" should not be read as "no bugs".
- Low overlap between tools (93% unique findings in the practitioner study) weakly suggests that two different reviewers find different things — consistent with cross-harness review, though the sample is one codebase.
- Noise has measurable costs (Beko +2.5h PR closure; Meta +5% review time; Mozilla developers edited 30% of accepted comments), so each extra review round must earn its latency.

### Gaps
- No Microsoft field study of LLM review-comment acceptance was found in this pass (Microsoft work located was on Copilot PR descriptions and CodeReviewer pretraining, not comment acceptance).
- No published study ties a specific acceptance-rate threshold to developer abandonment; the 80% (Google) and "precision first" (Uber) figures are operating targets, not measured abandonment points.
- Vendor tools (CodeRabbit, Greptile, Bugbot, Copilot) publish no audited precision/recall; the only numbers are from a single-codebase practitioner comparison and GitHub's own product metrics.

## KQ4: Iteration — how many refinement rounds help, and what happens to diff size and scope under repeated fixing?

### Takeaway
Across self-refinement, self-repair, debate and UI-critique studies, most of the benefit arrives in the first one or two rounds; further rounds add cost, raise false-positive rates, and can lower accuracy. Iterative fixing also inflates patches well beyond developer-written fixes, and "plausible" patches that pass visible tests frequently diverge behaviorally from correct ones, so growing diffs under review pressure are a documented risk rather than a hypothetical.

### Cited Findings
- Self-Refine iteration curve, Code Optimization: 22.0 (initial) -> 27.0 (iter 1) -> 27.9 (iter 2) -> 28.8 (iter 3); "most gains are in the initial iterations for both Code Opt. and Sentiment Reversal". — [ar5iv 2303.17651](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.17651)
- Huang et al.: round 2 of intrinsic self-correction was equal or worse than round 1 in 5 of 6 model-task cells (e.g., GPT-4 GSM8K 91.5% -> 89.0%; GPT-4 HotpotQA 49.0% -> 43.0%). — [ar5iv 2310.01798](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.01798)
- "How Many Tries Does It Take? Iterative Self-Repair in LLM Code Generation" (2026): seven models from Llama 3.1 8B to Gemini 2.5 Pro on HumanEval and MBPP; five repair attempts gave +4.9 to +17.1 pp (HumanEval) and +16.0 to +30.0 pp (MBPP), but "most gains concentrate in the first two rounds" (snippet: two rounds capture 76-95% of achievable gains); assertion (logic) errors repair at only ~45% vs much higher for syntax/name errors. Preprint. — [arXiv 2604.10508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10508)
- Olausson et al.: at fixed budget, "increasing initial diverse samples consistently outperformed extensive repair iterations"; self-repair is "not uniformly better than a non-repair strategy, especially when the sample budget is low". — [arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09896v4)
- "The Art of Repair" (EASE 2025): with a 10-patch budget on HumanEval-Java and Defects4J (DeepSeekCoder-, CodeLlama-, Llama3.1-Instruct), iterative generation beat one-shot for base models, but "exceeding certain thresholds leads to diminishing outcomes, likely due to overfitting". Peer-reviewed. — [arXiv 2505.02931](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02931)
- Reflexion: AlfWorld showed "an immediate spike in the improvement between the first two trials" then slower gains over 11 more trials; HumanEval memory was bounded to 1-3 stored reflections. — [ar5iv 2303.11366](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.11366)
- Du et al.: debate accuracy rose with rounds but "plateaued after 4 rounds". — [ar5iv 2305.14325](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.14325)
- "More Rounds, More Noise" (2026): additional review rounds raised recall +0.08 but increased false positives 62% and cut precision from 0.30 to 0.20, driven by reviewers inventing findings once real ones were exhausted. — [arXiv 2603.16244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16244)
- CHI 2024 UI-feedback study: GPT-4 feedback accuracy fell from 52% in the first round to 39% after designers applied edits, and helpfulness from 47% to 33%, making the tool "unsuitable for sustained iterative refinement". — [arXiv 2403.13139](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.13139)
- "Refine After Generation" (2026): across 28 SWE-bench approaches, successful LLM patches exceeded gold patches by a median +121.78% total changes, +80.91% net changes, +13.06% edited files and +43.99% cyclomatic complexity (snippet: iterative-refinement systems +406.46% total changes vs +95.60% for non-iterative); a post-hoc refinement step (RECAP) cut average total changes from +242.14% to +4.24% while "preserving or improving resolution by up to 42 instances". Preprint. — [arXiv 2608.13292](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13292)
- Wang, Pradel & Liu (ICSE 2026), "Are 'Solved Issues' in SWE-bench Really Solved Correctly?": 7.8% of patches counted correct fail the developer-written tests; 29.6% of plausible patches behave differently from the ground truth, due to "similar but divergent implementations" (46.8%) and patches that "adapt more behavior than the ground truth" (27.3%); reported resolution rates were inflated by 6.2 pp. Peer-reviewed. — [arXiv 2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223); DOI 10.1145/3744916.3764576
- Beko field study: developers rejected AI comments that "fix code blocks that are not in the scope of the task" and reported "recursive redundant comments". — [arXiv 2412.18531](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.18531)
- Claude Code docs (vendor): "If you've corrected Claude more than twice on the same issue in one session, the context is cluttered with failed approaches. Run /clear and start fresh"; and the reviewer callout on over-engineering quoted in KQ3. — [code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)
- Secondary summary of the 2026 harness-ablation paper (dev.to, weak): a structured plan "gave the agent an explicit stopping condition, preventing it from spiraling into speculative rewrites after passing the target test suite". — [dev.to](https://dev.to/reidmarlow/the-coding-agent-harness-paper-finally-ran-component-ablations-1n39); primary paper [arXiv 2609.20804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804)

### Inferences
- Two review-fix rounds capture most of the available gain; a third is marginal; beyond that, false positives and overfitting dominate. A loop that stops when the count of accepted findings stops falling is consistent with this, but should also carry a hard cap (3 rounds is the defensible default) because the reviewer's finding count does not fall to zero on its own ("false positive pressure").
- Diff growth is the expected side effect of iterative fixing; the loop should measure net lines/files touched per round against the task scope and treat growth without new failing tests as a signal to stop or revert, not as progress.
- Passing visible tests is weak evidence of correctness (7.8% / 29.6%); the reviewer's most valuable contribution is behavioral divergence and over-broad changes, which tests miss.

### Gaps
- No study measures iteration counts for an author-reviewer pair of different frontier models on repository-scale tasks; the round-count evidence comes from single-model self-repair and from UI critique.
- SWE-agent-style papers report step budgets but I found no ablation isolating "number of review-fix cycles" as a variable.

## KQ5: Plan-first and spec-first — does writing and reviewing a plan before code improve agent outcomes?

### Takeaway
Planning before coding reliably helps weaker models and competitive-programming-style tasks (up to +25% relative pass@1; PlanSearch 41.4% -> 77.0% with search), and in 2026 agent studies it improves SWE-bench resolution modestly (+1 to +5 pp) and mainly saves cost for strong models; a bad plan hurts more than no plan, which is the strongest argument for reviewing plans rather than just writing them. Vendor guidance (Anthropic) endorses plan mode for uncertain or multi-file changes and advises skipping it for one-sentence diffs. Measured developer-productivity effects of AI tooling overall remain uncertain (METR: -19% in early 2025; weak-evidence 2026 update suggesting a reversal).

### Cited Findings
- Jiang et al. (TOSEM), "Self-planning Code Generation with LLMs": planning steps first, then step-wise implementation, gave up to 25.4% relative Pass@1 improvement over direct generation and 11.9% over chain-of-thought across multiple benchmarks and languages. Peer-reviewed. — [arXiv 2303.06689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06689)
- Wang et al. (2024), PlanSearch: searching over natural-language plans raised Claude 3.5 Sonnet on LiveCodeBench from 41.4% pass@1 to 77.0% pass@200 (vs 60.6% for plain repeated sampling), with gains predicted by idea diversity. Preprint. — [arXiv 2409.03733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.03733)
- PMCoder (2026): coupling planning with episodic memory on SWE-bench Verified gave +5.0 pp (+25 cases) over baseline, +2.8 pp across models including Claude Haiku 4.5 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash; snippet ablation: baseline 28.5%, plan-only 29.7%, memory-only 30.1%, both 33.5%. Preprint. — [arXiv 2608.06811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06811)
- "An Empirical Study of Harness Design for Coding Agents" (2026): across 4 models on SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.1, "planning shifts from an accuracy scaffold for weaker models to a cost saver for stronger models, with little change in accuracy". Preprint. — [arXiv 2609.20804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804)
- "From Plan to Action: How Well Do Agents Follow the Plan?" (2026): 4 LLMs via SWE-agent, 21,120 trajectories on SWE-bench Verified/Pro; "providing the standard plan improves issue resolution", "periodic plan reminders can mitigate plan violations", but "a subpar plan hurts performance even more than no plan at all" and inserting extra early phases misaligned with the model's internal strategy can degrade results. Preprint. — [arXiv 2604.12147](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12147)
- RigorBench (2026): five process pillars (Planning Fidelity, Verification Coverage, Recovery Efficiency, Abstention Quality, Atomic Transition Integrity); structured process discipline raised process-quality scores by 41% and "downstream outcome correctness by 17%"; snippet: planning fidelity rose +0.57 under structured prompting from a near-absent baseline. Preprint. — [arXiv 2606.22678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22678)
- Anthropic Claude Code best practices (vendor): "Letting Claude jump straight to coding can produce code that solves the wrong problem"; recommended Explore -> Plan -> Implement -> Commit; "Plan mode is useful, but also adds overhead ... If you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan"; for larger features, "have Claude interview you ... then write a complete spec to SPEC.md" and "start a fresh session to execute it". No quantitative claim. — [code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)
- METR RCT (2025): 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks, Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet; AI-allowed tasks took 19% longer; developers forecast 24% speedup and estimated 20% speedup afterwards; economists/ML experts predicted 38-39%. Strong but small-N. — [arXiv 2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089); [metr.org](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
- METR update (Feb 2026): 57 developers, 143 repos, 800+ tasks; returning developers estimated 18% speedup (CI from 38% speedup to 9% slowdown), new developers 4% (CI 15% speedup to 9% slowdown); METR calls this "only very weak evidence" because developers refused to work without AI and 30-50% withheld tasks they expected AI to accelerate. — [metr.org](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- Spec-driven development claims ("3-10x higher first-pass success") appear only in vendor/consultancy blogs with no methodology. Vendor/unverified. — [devtoollab.com](https://devtoollab.com/blog/spec-driven-development-ai-agents)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports a plan step for multi-file or ambiguous tasks and a plan *review* specifically because plan quality has asymmetric payoff: a flawed plan is worse than none. A cross-harness plan review is a cheap insurance against the expensive failure mode.
- For strong models on well-scoped tasks, planning buys cost control and a stopping condition more than accuracy; the plan-review skill should be skippable for small diffs, matching Anthropic's own guidance.
- Plan adherence drifts during long runs; re-injecting the plan (or reviewing the diff against the plan, as the Claude docs suggest) is supported by the plan-reminder result.

### Gaps
- No study measures the effect of a *second model reviewing the plan* (as opposed to the author planning) on outcome quality; the inference above rests on "subpar plan hurts" plus the KQ2 reviewer-bias evidence.
- Industry (OpenAI/Anthropic) publish no controlled data on plan-first vs direct coding; only guidance.

## KQ6: Reliability of vision-language-model judgments of UI screenshots for layout defects

### Takeaway
Frontier VLMs are now reliable for coarse visual judgments of a screenshot (aesthetics/design quality agreement with experts KBP >= 0.81 for GPT-5/Claude 4 Sonnet in a 2026 study) and useful for guided defect detection when the screenshot is annotated (Set-of-Mark occlusion detection F1 87.2%), but they remain weak at exactly the primitives layout review needs: deciding whether close elements overlap or touch, counting, reading small text, pixel-level color/contrast, high-resolution small targets, and interaction-dependent usability. Unaided zero-shot critique precision has been low (13.1% valid for Gemini Pro Vision in UICrit; 52% accurate for GPT-4 on structured UI JSON) and decays across iterations.

### Cited Findings
- Design2Code (NAACL 2025): 484 real webpages; human annotators judged GPT-4V-generated pages exchangeable with the original in 49% of cases and better-designed in 64%; GPT-4V (text-augmented) scored Block-Match 87.6, Text 98.2, Position 80.2, Color 73.0, CLIP 87.2; models "mostly lag in recalling visual elements from the input webpages and generating correct layout designs". Strong. — [arXiv 2403.03163](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03163); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.03163)
- UICrit (UIST 2024): 3,059 expert critiques on 983 mobile UIs from 7 designers (topics: layout 696, color contrast 655, text readability 591, button usability 601, learnability 601); with Gemini Pro Vision, only 13.1% of zero-shot generated comments were valid without filtering; few-shot/visual prompting raised the normalized quality score from 0.31 to 0.48 (the "55% gain"); "LLMs have poor object localization", producing inaccurate bounding boxes. Strong, but an older model. — [arXiv 2407.08850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08850); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08850)
- Duan et al. (CHI 2024), "Generating Automatic Feedback on UI Mockups with LLMs": GPT-4 given a JSON rendering of Figma mockups (not a screenshot); on 51 UIs, 52% of suggestions were rated accurate and 49% helpful by three experts; accuracy fell to 39% and helpfulness to 33% in later iterations; failure modes included "misdetecting overlaps based on bounding boxes when elements don't visually overlap", failing to recognize center alignment of different-sized elements, over-literal guideline application, repeated identical feedback, and missing global issues; 9 of 12 designers would still use it. Strong. — [arXiv 2403.13139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13139); full text [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.13139)
- "Visual Prompting with Iterative Refinement for Design Critique Generation" (2024/25): Gemini 1.5 Pro and GPT-4o produce critiques with bounding boxes from a screenshot plus guidelines; the pipeline "reduc[ed] the gap from human performance by 50% for one rating metric" and was preferred over the baseline by human raters. Preprint. — [arXiv 2412.16829](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16829)
- Khalil & Rehman (HCI International 2026, best paper), "Can Multimodal LLMs Model Expert Ratings in Mobile UI Usability Evaluation?": GPT-5, Claude 4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen-3 VL on 1,000 UICrit screens; on visual dimensions (Aesthetics, Design Quality) three models reached "almost perfect" agreement (KBP >= 0.81) zero-shot; on functional dimensions (Usability, Learnability, Efficiency) models over-estimated by +1.00 to +3.00 points (p<0.001); GPT-5 strongest (KBP > 0.60 on most dimensions), Gemini 2.5 Pro weakest (KBP < 0.42 functional); few-shot prompting "generally worsened agreement" by 0.01-0.27. Preprint. — [arXiv 2510.08783](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08783v1)

Peer-reviewed. — [DOI 10.1145/3744257.3744270](https://doi.org/10.1145/3744257.3744270)

### Inferences
- Screenshot review by a frontier VLM is trustworthy for "does this look like the design / is the visual hierarchy right / is anything obviously broken" and untrustworthy as the sole detector of the specific defects the project names: overlap of adjacent elements, truncation of small text, exact contrast ratios, and responsive breakage that depends on fine spatial relations. False negatives on these are expected at the 25-60% level based on the primitive-task results.
- Reliability improves materially with structure: marked-up screenshots (Set-of-Mark), element metadata (DOM/accessibility tree, bounding boxes, computed styles), and narrowly specified defect classes. Deterministic checks (axe-core contrast, overflow/scroll-width scripts, viewport matrix) should produce the facts and the VLM should interpret them.
- Critique quality drops across iterations and few-shot examples can hurt current frontier models on rating tasks, so the design-review skill should be one careful pass with rich inputs rather than repeated loops.
- Do not use a single VLM reviewer's "approval" as evidence of correctness; use it to find candidates, then confirm each with a measurement or a human look.

### Gaps
- No benchmark measures VLM precision/recall specifically for web responsive breakage (e.g., horizontal overflow at 375px) or text truncation on desktop web pages; the closest are mobile multi-window defects (2026) and generic primitive tasks.
- No study evaluates a VLM reviewing screenshots of code written by a *different* agent for design conformance; design-review cross-harness evidence is absent and must be inferred from KQ2 plus the reliability data above.

## What the evidence implies for the project's design choices

### Takeaway
The repository's central bet — a different harness checks each stage, with a reviewer that only reports must-fix findings and a loop that stops when accepted findings stop falling — is well aligned with the strongest findings (self-review is weak, same-family judges are biased, precision drives adoption, gains concentrate in rounds 1-2); the main refinements the evidence suggests are a hard round cap, scope/diff-growth guards, test-anchored arbitration of disputed findings, and treating screenshot review as candidate-generation over structured inputs rather than a verdict.

### Cited Findings
- Cross-harness review over self-review: self-correction degrades without external signals ([Huang 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798); [Kamoi 2406.01297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)); judges favor own and same-family outputs even on verifiable code rubrics ([2604.06996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06996)); cross-family verifiers accept fewer wrong answers ([2512.02304](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02304v2)); a stronger different model's feedback unlocked repair that self-feedback could not ([Olausson 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09896v4)); Uber's best F1 came from a Claude generator plus an OpenAI grader ([uber.com](https://www.uber.com/en-GB/blog/ureview/)).
- Fresh context matters independently of model identity: independent-session review beat multi-turn review on F1 (0.376 vs 0.303) ([2603.16244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16244)); Anthropic's docs say a fresh context avoids bias toward code just written ([code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).
- Must-fix-only filtering: Google required 80% useful before rollout and reached it only by suppressing categories ([2405.13565](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13565)); Uber suppresses low-reception categories because false positives make engineers "tune out" ([uber.com](https://www.uber.com/en-GB/blog/ureview/)); unfiltered functional comments were accepted ~5% of the time ([2411.07091](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07091)); Anthropic warns reviewers "will usually report some [gaps], even when the work is sound" ([code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).
- Stop rule and round cap: gains concentrate in the first two rounds ([2604.10508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10508); [Self-Refine](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.17651)); more rounds raise false positives 62% ([2603.16244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16244)); iterative systems inflate patches (+406% vs +96% snippet; median +121.78% over gold) ([2608.13292](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13292)); out-of-scope fixes are the top reason developers ignore AI comments ([2412.18531](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.18531)).
- Arbitration: authors are sycophantic to pushback ([2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)) and critics hallucinate bugs at rates "much higher" than humans ([2407.00215](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00215)); human+critic teams hallucinate less; plausible patches diverge behaviorally 29.6% of the time ([2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15223)).
- Plan review: a subpar plan hurts more than no plan ([2604.12147](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12147)); planning is an accuracy scaffold for weaker models and a cost saver for strong ones ([2609.20804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20804)); skip planning for one-sentence diffs ([code.claude.com](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)).
- Design review: frontier VLMs match experts on aesthetics (KBP >= 0.81) but not usability ([hci.international](https://2026.hci.international/MOBILE-best-paper-award.html)); they miss overlap/counting/small-target primitives at 25-60% rates ([2407.06581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06581); [2504.07981](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07981)); structured inputs lift occlusion detection to F1 87.2% ([2604.19081](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19081)); critique quality decays across iterations ([2403.13139](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.13139)).

### Inferences
- Keep the cross-harness architecture; it is the configuration the bias and verification literature favors, and prefer cross-vendor over cross-model-same-vendor because family-level bias is documented.
- Make the reviewer stateless per round (fresh context, sees only diff + criteria + plan), and have it classify findings as must-fix only when they affect correctness or stated requirements; expect and accept low finding counts.
- Add to the "stop when accepted findings stop falling" rule: (a) a hard cap of three review-fix rounds, (b) a diff-growth check (net lines/files vs round 0 and vs task scope) that halts the loop when the diff grows without a new failing test, and (c) a rule that disputed findings are settled by a reproducing test or measurement, not by argument.
- Treat "no must-fix findings" as necessary, not sufficient: recall of AI reviewers on human-flagged issues is 15-31%, so tests, builds and behavioral diff checks remain the primary gate.
- For plan review, prioritize detecting a *wrong* plan (wrong files, missing verification step, scope creep) over polishing a good one, and allow skipping for small diffs.
- For design review, feed the reviewer annotated screenshots across a viewport matrix plus DOM/accessibility-tree and computed-style facts, request specific defect classes, run deterministic contrast/overflow checks first, and run one pass rather than a loop; present VLM findings to the user as candidates with screenshot evidence.
- Instrument the skills: log findings per round, acceptance rate, diff growth and time added, because field data shows the same tool class can land anywhere from 7% to 75% acceptance and from -3h to +10h PR cycle time depending on policy.

### Gaps
- There is no controlled study of Claude-reviews-Codex or Codex-reviews-Claude on real repositories; the project's own logs would be the first such dataset.
- No published acceptance-rate threshold predicts when developers abandon an AI reviewer; the 80% (Google) target is an operating choice.
- VLM reliability for desktop-web responsive defects specifically is unmeasured.
